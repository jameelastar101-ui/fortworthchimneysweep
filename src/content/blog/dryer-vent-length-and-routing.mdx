## Dryer vent length and routing at a glance

The commonly quoted code limit for a dryer vent is 35 feet from the dryer to the outside, minus 5 feet for each 90-degree bend and 2.5 feet for each 45-degree bend. Your dryer's manual can set a different limit. Keep the run short, straight, and easy to reach for cleaning.

| Bends in the run | Straight duct allowed |
|---|---|
| None | 35 feet |
| One 45-degree bend | 32.5 feet |
| Two 45-degree bends | 30 feet |
| One 90-degree bend | 30 feet |
| Two 90-degree bends | 25 feet |
| Three 90-degree bends | 20 feet |
| Four 90-degree bends | 15 feet |

The table starts from the 35-foot baseline. Your dryer manual may allow less or more.

## How is dryer vent length measured?

The length is the total duct from the dryer to the outside, plus an allowance for each bend. Code calls this the developed length, and the <a href="https://www.nachi.org/dryer-vent-safety.htm" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">International Residential Code rule, as summarized by InterNACHI</a>, sets the 35-foot maximum.

Count every foot of straight duct, vertical sections included. Then add 5 feet for each 90-degree bend and 2.5 feet for each 45-degree bend.

A run with 18 feet of straight duct, two 90-degree bends, and one 45-degree bend counts as 30.5 feet.

A run with 22 feet of straight duct and three 90-degree bends counts as 37 feet. That is over the 35-foot limit.

## Why can your dryer's manual set a different limit than code?

The code defers to the manufacturer's installation instructions, so the manual's limit controls. A manual can allow a shorter or a longer run than 35 feet.

When in doubt, use the shorter number. Some websites quote other limits, such as 25 feet, so check your manual.

## What is the transition duct, and how long can it be?

The transition duct is the short connector between the back of the dryer and the duct in the wall. It is limited to 8 feet, and it is not counted in the 35-foot length.

Keep it visible and reachable. Do not hide it inside a wall.

## Why do long and bent runs clog faster?

Every added foot and every elbow slows the airflow, which lets lint settle. <a href="https://producthelp.whirlpool.com/Laundry/Dryers/Product_Info/Dryer_Product_Assistance/How_to_Check_Venting" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">Whirlpool's venting guidance</a> says added length and elbows reduce airflow and encourage lint buildup.

A run longer than the manufacturer's chart allows lengthens drying times and raises energy use. The signs are in the guide to the [signs of a clogged dryer vent](/signs-of-a-clogged-dryer-vent/).

## Where can a dryer vent end?

A dryer vent must end outdoors, through a wall hood or a roof cap. It cannot end inside the house or in a space inside the building.

### Places a dryer vent cannot end

Model residential codes do not allow dryer exhaust to end in an attic, crawl space, soffit, or ridge vent. Hot, moist air and lint then collect inside the building.

### Rules for the exterior hood

The hood needs a backdraft damper. It should not have a screen, because a screen catches lint.

The hood should sit at least 3 feet from any opening into the building. Whirlpool also advises keeping it at least 12 inches from the ground and from plants, rocks, or snow.

## Roof vs. wall dryer vent: which is better?

A wall vent is easier to inspect and clean. A roof vent is often shorter for an upstairs or interior laundry room. Neither is automatically better, because the right choice depends on where your laundry room sits.

| Factor | Wall vent | Roof vent |
|---|---|---|
| Path length | Short when the laundry room is on an exterior wall | Often shorter for an interior or upstairs laundry room |
| Inspection | Visible from the ground or a ladder | Needs roof access |
| Cleaning | Easier to reach | Harder to reach |
| Lint at the outlet | Easy to spot at the hood | Can collect at the roof cap |
| Leak risk | No roof opening | The roof opening can leak if poorly installed |

### Path length

An exterior-wall laundry room needs only a short wall run. An interior or upstairs room may need a long horizontal run to reach a wall, while a vertical run to the roof can be much shorter.

### Inspection

You can check a wall hood from the ground. A roof vent needs roof access, so most homeowners never check it.

### Cleaning

A wall terminal is easy to reach. A roof terminal is harder to reach, which makes a professional the safer choice for a full cleaning.

### Lint at the outlet

<a href="https://structuretech.com/dont-vent-your-clothes-dryer-through-the-roof/" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">Structure Tech</a> reports that roof dryer terminals clog with lint and are difficult to clean. A wall hood shows a lint problem sooner because you can see it.

### Leak risk

A roof opening lets water in when the flashing or cap is poor. A wall vent has no roof opening.

## Dryer vent routing in multi-story homes

Many newer two-story homes put the laundry room on the second floor, often inside the house instead of against an exterior wall, according to <a href="https://structuretech.com/dont-vent-your-clothes-dryer-through-the-roof/" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">Structure Tech</a>. That layout forces the vent to travel up into the attic or down and out.

The best route is the shortest, straightest path that still ends outdoors. For an interior upstairs laundry room, that is often a vertical run through the attic and roof.

Keep the duct reachable for cleaning. Do not run it farther than the layout requires.

## What to check on a dryer vent that runs through the attic

Concealed sections need smooth rigid metal duct. Flexible foil or vinyl duct does not belong in an attic.

The duct should be supported, and every joint should be sealed. You should be able to reach the run for cleaning.

Leave attic work to a professional.

## When does a booster fan help?

A booster fan helps when a long run cannot be shortened. It is a fallback, not a first choice.

Use only a unit listed for dryer exhaust, and follow both the fan maker's and the dryer maker's instructions. Model codes call these dryer exhaust duct power ventilators.

## How can you shorten a dryer vent run?

Shorten the run by removing length and bends, not by adding power.

- Move the dryer closer to an exterior wall.
- Reroute the duct to use fewer bends.
- Replace sharp elbows with large-radius elbows, which can carry a smaller length penalty.
- Shorten the transition duct.

## How to measure your own dryer vent run

Measure only what you can reach safely. The steps below give a rough total you can compare to your limit.

<OptionalImage
  src="/images/blog/dryer-vent-length-and-routing/dryer-vent-measure-your-run-infographic.webp"
  alt="Infographic outlining 6 steps to measure your dryer vent run, from checking your manual to comparing the total to your limit"
  width={1376}
  height={768}
/>

1. **Find the limit in your dryer manual.** Look in the venting section for the maximum length. Use the shorter of that number and 35 feet.
2. **Follow the duct from the back of the dryer to the outside.** Note where it passes through a wall, ceiling, attic, or roof.
3. **Measure each straight section you can see.** If a section is hidden in a wall or attic, estimate it from the floor plan or ask a professional.
4. **Count the bends you can see.** Hidden bends make the real total higher than your count.
5. **Add the allowances.** Add 5 feet for each 90-degree bend and 2.5 feet for each 45-degree bend.
6. **Compare the total to your limit.** A total over the limit, or a run you cannot fully see, calls for a professional.

Longer, bent, attic, and roof runs belong on the 6-month end of the schedule in the guide to [how often to clean a dryer vent](/how-often-to-clean-a-dryer-vent/). A roof terminal is also harder to check from the ground, so ask a professional to inspect it.

## Which dryers do not need an exhaust vent?

Condensing and heat pump dryers do not vent outdoors. They collect the moisture instead.

Your manual says which type you own. Do not disconnect or seal a vent on a dryer that needs one.

Some Fort Worth homes route the dryer vent through the attic to a roof exit. Homes with an interior or upstairs laundry room often need longer runs than homes with the dryer on an exterior wall.
